Measles in plain words

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases there is, and it travels through the air.

When someone with measles breathes, coughs or sneezes, the virus can float in the air of a room for up to two hours after they leave. About 9 in 10 people who are not protected and share that air will catch it. It starts like a bad cold: fever, cough, runny nose and red, watery eyes. A rash follows a few days later.

Most people recover, but measles can cause pneumonia, brain swelling and death, especially in babies and young children. In 2026 the United States has had more than 5,000 confirmed cases, the most since 1991, and in November it lost the measles elimination status it had held since 2000.

The two windows

After an exposure, two kinds of protection can still help, but only for a short time.

The MMR vaccine, given within 72 hours of your first exposure, can prevent measles or make it milder.

Immune globulin, given within 6 days, gives short-term protection to people who cannot have the vaccine or missed the 72 hours: babies under 12 months, pregnant people who are not immune, and people with weak immune systems.

The clock starts when you were exposed, not when you hear about it. That is why every hour of a late notice matters.

Am I protected?

Most adults are, but you need proof, not a guess.

You are usually protected if you have written proof of two doses of MMR vaccine, a blood test showing immunity, a past case of measles confirmed by a lab, or you were born before 1957. If you are protected, you do not need a shot after an exposure, but you should still watch for symptoms. If you are not sure, ask your doctor; an extra dose of MMR is safe.

If you were exposed

Act on what is still open, then watch.

  1. Check your vaccine record.
  2. If you are not protected and are within 72 hours, call your doctor, a pharmacy or the health department about the MMR vaccine.
  3. If you are pregnant, have a baby, or have a weak immune system, ask about immune globulin. It can help up to 6 days after exposure.
  4. Watch for fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes and a rash from day 5 to day 21.
  5. If symptoms start, call before you go in, so the clinic can keep you away from other patients. Do not walk into a waiting room unannounced.

Why notices arrive late

A notice can only go out after someone is diagnosed, tested and traced.

Someone gets sick days after they were contagious. They see a doctor, a lab confirms measles, and the health department interviews them to find every place they went. Only then is a notice published. Seventy-Two measures the delay from the notices it reads.

How we measure it. For each exposure we take the start of the exposure window and the date the notice was published, from dated press releases only. List pages that collect many exposures only tell us when the page was last updated, so they do not count toward the delay. When several departments report the same exposure, we count it once, using the earliest notice. Publication dates are given as days, not hours, so each delay is accurate to within a day.

How Seventy-Two works

It reads the notices for you, checks them against where you were, and keeps your deadlines.

Reading notices

Every 30 minutes Seventy-Two opens every health department page it watches. If a page has not changed, it stops there. If it has, it keeps a copy, and Claude Haiku, an AI model, reads it and lists every place and time it gives, each with the exact words from the notice. Code then checks every one: the quote must really appear in the notice, its date must be written in or just above the quote, the place must be named within a few lines, and each time must be inside the quote. A window whose date is not there is dropped and counted; a time that is not there is removed, so the whole day counts. If a notice gives no clock times, we say so and count the whole day; we never invent a time.

Checking where you were

You describe your trip in your own words. Claude turns it into places and times. Code then decides whether your times overlap a notice's times and whether the places are the same, and works out your deadlines from the CDC's rules. The AI never decides whether you were exposed or what your deadline is.

Watching your trip

Most notices go up days after the exposure, so "no match" today can mean "no notice yet". Press Watch this trip and Seventy-Two keeps your places and times until 21 days after your last stop. Every time it stores a new exposure window, it checks that window against your trip straight away. If one matches, a notification arrives on every device where you pressed Alert me on this device, your trip page shows what to do, and your calendar adds an event if you subscribed to the trip's feed. You can stop watching and delete the trip at any time.

Your clock

When you start a clock, AWS starts a workflow just for you. Each deadline is a step that waits until its moment. A day before the vaccine and immune globulin deadlines it reminds you, then waits for you to say you got the shot. On day 5 it reminds you to watch for symptoms, and on day 21 it tells you the watch has ended. While it waits, it costs nothing. You can also add every deadline to your own calendar, so you get reminders even if you never come back.

How it is built, for engineers

Ten serverless AWS services do the work, with no servers, no deployment pipeline, and one command to deploy.

Every 30 minutes

  1. EventBridge Scheduler starts the read workflow.
  2. Step Functions runs a Distributed Map over the source list in S3: each source is a short Express child, six at a time, retried with backoff, and the run carries on past failures.
  3. For each source, Lambda fetches the page and hashes its text. Unchanged pages stop here, at no model cost.
  4. On a department's news page or feed, every new measles link is read as a new notice, three a run, newest first.
  5. Changed pages are archived to S3 by hash. Claude Haiku 4.5 on Bedrock proposes windows through a forced tool call; code checks that each quote, date and time is written in the notice.
  6. Windows go to DynamoDB with deterministic ids, so re-reading never duplicates.
  7. A snapshot of the board is written to S3 for failover. Every function reports what it did as metrics and traces, shown on the Ops page.

When you check

  1. CloudFront serves the site from S3 and sends API calls to API Gateway, which throttles every route.
  2. Lambda asks Claude to turn your words into stops, then decides overlap and deadlines in plain code from CDC rules.
  3. Reports of the same exposure from several departments are merged into one.
  4. If the API fails, CloudFront serves the last board snapshot from S3 instead.
  5. A saved trip is matched by DynamoDB Streams: each new window goes to a matcher function the moment it is stored. It reads only the stops saved in that state on those days, and sends a Web Push notification on a match. With 10,000 saved trips, an airport exposure was matched to all 300 that passed through in about 2 seconds.

Your clock

  1. One Step Functions Standard workflow per person, started by the API.
  2. A Map runs over the list of deadlines in the disease's rule file. Wait states hold each one; a task token pauses the workflow until you press "I got it", or times out at the deadline.
  3. Each step writes what happened to DynamoDB; the clock page reads the live execution history.
  4. The clock's record carries a delete date. DynamoDB removes it on its own: the privacy policy is a column.

Every service, library and tool, with how each is set up, is on the stack page. The code, the template and the deploy script are in the project repository. Deploying is one command, python scripts/deploy.py, from a laptop or AWS CloudShell.

A second disease, same stack

Everything about measles is in one rule file. Hepatitis A is a second one, and it runs here today.

A rule file says what a notice is, which actions protect a person and how long each window lasts, how long to watch for symptoms, and the words a person reads. In the Lab, choose hepatitis A: the same reader and the same checks read a real New York City notice about a restaurant worker, and a demo clock runs the 2-week vaccine and immune globulin deadlines on the same Step Functions workflow as every measles clock. Any notice that starts a personal deadline, such as a product recall or a boil-water notice, fits the same pattern.

The clock also comes on its own, with no measles, Claude or website, as one template in the project repository (patterns/deadline-clock/) for any app with deadlines. Each reminder carries a one-click link for the person to say they acted. On AWS, with a shop's refund clocks, 4 of 4 passed: the deadlines the customers met through the link ended as acted, and the ones they ignored closed themselves 0.2 to 0.6 seconds after they passed. One-time EventBridge Scheduler schedules, measured the same way, fired up to 50 seconds late.

Privacy

We keep only what you type, and only while it can still matter.

  • No accounts, no location tracking, no cookies for tracking.
  • The text of a check is used to answer it and is not stored.
  • To keep the free service fair, we count requests per connection per day. The counter stores a scrambled (hashed) form of your network address, never the address itself, and deletes itself after two days.
  • A saved trip is kept until 21 days after its last stop, then deletes itself. You can delete it sooner from its page. Its address is the only key to it.
  • A clock is stored until the day after its watch ends, then deletes itself. Demo clocks delete themselves after two days.
  • Anything you paste into the Lab deletes itself after one day and is never shown to anyone else.
  • Notices we read are public documents; we keep copies so every window can be checked against its source.
  • Metrics and traces describe the stack, not you: counts, timings, costs and window ids. A link you paste into the Lab is traced as "a link pasted in the Lab", never by its address.

What it does not do

It is a reminder and a reader, not a doctor.

Seventy-Two does not diagnose, give medical advice or know your vaccine history. It reads a growing list of health departments, not all of them; the live status on the board lists every source and when it was last read. If your notice is not there, paste it into the Lab.
